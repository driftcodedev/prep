How many calories will my fyke nets catch?

For fykes set in a Cobscook stream during the May–June alewife run. Enter your people, your stream and your nets, and see how much of your food the catch covers.

The fish: alewives (river herring). Blueback herring run with them and are counted together. Adults are about 11–12 in long and weigh about 8 oz. They come up the streams from early May to early June, and most pass in about two weeks.

About 17 calories per lb a day, for hard daily work.

Year

On Maine's counted runs, the worst year of the last 11 was half to nine-tenths of a typical year. Small runs can fall further.

Nets

Fish columns count alewives; calories columns are food. % of need compares with what your people need a day. Safe to eat stops at 36% of need: alewives are lean, and eating more would make too much of your diet protein. Fish caught beyond that are extra, to release or keep in reserve.

Peak: about 80% of a run passes in roughly two weeks. "Calories caught a day" spreads the season's catch over all 61 days of May–June, which means storing it.

How it works

Share one net takes = 12% × part of the stream it blocks × part of the depth it fills

  • The 12% comes from two records. In the Fore River, Massachusetts (2009–2012), a small fyke with two 4 ft wings sat in a 45 ft channel with a full-width net behind it. It caught 1,623 smelt to the big net's 47,736: 3.3% of the fish passing, while blocking at most a quarter of the channel. That's 12.5% of the fish in the part it blocked, or more if its wings were angled or the water was deeper than the net. On the Margaree River, Nova Scotia, alewife traps that filter half the river each took about 3–8% of the fish passing them: 6.5–16% of the fish in their half. The middle of where the two agree is about 12–15%; the calculator uses 12%.
  • Longer wings: the calculator assumes catch grows with the share of the stream blocked. Studies of wing length mostly support that for fish on the move (doubling a small fyke's lead tripled its catch in one), but not every study found a difference.
  • Night to night, catches swing a lot: the Fore River fyke caught anywhere from under 1% to 19% as many fish as the big net in a given week. Season totals are much steadier than single days.
  • Bad years may hurt less than the run size suggests. On the Margaree, alewives swam in small schools near the banks when runs were small, and spread across the river when runs were big. So a trap set from the bank took a bigger share of a small run.
  • Bad years: on Maine's counted runs and on Nova Scotia's Gaspereau River, the worst year was about half of a typical year.
  • Calories needed: about 17 per lb of body weight a day for hard daily work, from a standard formula for a 30-year-old, 5 ft 10 in man (3,100 a day at 180 lb). Women and lighter work need less.
  • The protein limit: spawning alewives are lean, about 69% of their calories from protein. A review of protein intake suggests keeping protein to about 25% of calories and calls over 35% dangerous. So alewives can safely be about 36% of your calories.

What it doesn't cover

  • Blocking more than three-quarters of the stream. Fish can't easily swim around, so the catch could be far higher, but neither record covers it. It also stops the run, which hurts next year's.
  • Other seasons. This is the alewife run only. Smelt (March–May) add about 320 calories a night per net, and they count toward the same protein limit.
  • Run sizes. The Dennys figure is a 2025 newspaper report of one million counted after fishway work at Meddybemps. The Pennamaquan was counted at nearly 500,000 in 2024, but a Passamaquoddy biologist said in 2025 that "the trend is down."
  • Floods, seals and people. Floods can lift a net, so anchor it well. Seals follow the runs: add a seal excluder. Endangered wild salmon in the Dennys must be let go.
  • Confidence: 3 out of 5 on either stream. The weakest link is the 12%: no study has measured a winged fyke against a counted alewife run, so it rests on a smelt fyke and alewife river traps.
